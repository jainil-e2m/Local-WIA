## Per-brief findings

### E: What goes into our decisions about competing venues (rank 1)

**W3 to W8 hits**
- W3, the subject line (no narration of a third party; WIA's counter-expected point): "We don't rule out a venue search by its name alone". The alternative "Should competing venues stay in your Google Ads?" is the reader's own question. Pass. "venue search" is slightly trade-flavoured.
- W5/W7, covers name the subject: "A competing venue's search needed context"; "Competing venues need local context". The subject is named, but both covers are somewhat abstract ("needed context"). On-screen hook: "Google Ads showed a venue couples also considered." This is strong and concrete.
- W4, slide 1 carries the title: the title and slide 1 are the same line, "Another venue in your Google Ads report needs context before exclusion." Pass.
- W6, insider terms: none in first-touch text. "Google Search Accelerator" appears in the body with its descriptor ("we build and launch your campaigns, and you run them with our experts beside you"). Pass.
- W8, b-roll a cold viewer can follow: "Carmen reviewed other venue names in a client's Google Ads." / "The client's team knew couples had considered one nearby venue as well as theirs." / "They placed another in a different market. Carmen agreed it should be excluded." The turn is followable. Screen 1 names "Carmen" to a cold viewer with no role, while the caption and email give it ("our Google Ads lead").

**Rule findings**
- 4.3 email skeleton complete: DATE, SEND TIME and RECIPIENTS, all proposed; subject plus two others; preview; an opening scene ("A member of a client's venue team gave Carmen a useful piece of context during a Google Ads review…"); one bold line ("**Another venue appearing in the account doesn't tell us whether its couples would also consider yours.**"); the offer with its descriptor; a reply CTA; "Talk soon, Whitney". No bullets and no "DM" in the email.
- Two Reels with a stated reason ("a third would repeat the same argument without adding a distinct creative door"). Reel 1 runs about 85 seconds and has no "Okay, so" opener.
- Carousel: titled, 9 slides, a bold belief slide, and slide 9 addresses both done-for-you and guided readers ("Some owners want us to manage… Others want us to launch… and guide them").
- 3.2/4.3 "who did what": the actors are exact. Carmen leads, Erin is "a member of the client's team", and the agreement is a recommendation, not a completed change. Fidelity is the best of the set.
- One Instagram offer CTA (on the carousel only). This complies.
- Small risks: the closing lines are mildly aphoristic ("That is how we want to work with venue teams: understanding the comparison before recommending what to rule out."). The alternative subject "Should competing venues stay in your Google Ads?" leans towards "I should change this in my account" (point test 3).

**Biggest weakness:** the covers and b-roll screen 1 are generic ("needed context", "Carmen reviewed…"), so the first touch is weaker than the hook and the email.

### B: How we decide whether to replace a client's existing page (rank 2)

**W3 to W8 hits**
- W3, subject: "A new landing page still has something to prove" states WIA's counter-expected stance and names the subject. The alternative "Does your current ad page need replacing?" is the reader's question. Pass.
- W5/W7, covers: "Our landing page wasn't an automatic replacement"; "A landing page has to earn the switch". Both are specific. The hook "We built a page. We didn't switch all the ad traffic." is strong.
- W4, slide 1: "A page we build should face the same scrutiny as the page you already use." It matches the carousel title. Pass, though it is phrased as a principle rather than an insight about the reader.
- W6, insider terms: none. ELEVATE appears once in the email, with its descriptor ("our full-service advertising management program").
- W8, b-roll: "A new landing page doesn't get all the ad traffic by default." / "We compared the page we built with the client's existing pages before replacing them." / "We then outlined changes to our version, with another client review planned." A cold viewer can follow it, but screen 3 is a flat process line, not a turn.

**Rule findings**
- 4.3 email skeleton complete: send header, subjects, preview, an opening scene in natural first person ("During a conversation with a client, I explained why we hadn't redirected all their ad traffic…"), a bold line ("**We owe our own work the same scrutiny we give the work a client brings to us.**"), the offer named with its descriptor, a reply CTA, "Talk soon, Whitney".
- Two Reels, with the missing stance Reel explained ("the narrow moment does not support another distinct treatment without repetition"). The personal reaction lines are flagged for Whitney. The script has a natural spoken beat ("A little context.").
- Carousel: titled, 7 slides, belief slide in bold, slide 7 for both kinds of reader.
- Fidelity: careful. The revisions stay in planned tense, and nothing claims a winning page.
- Voice: too abstract and repetitive in the captions: "scrutiny", "contribution", "consideration". There are aphoristic closers ("The same judgment should apply to what we create and what you bring to the partnership."; "without making a change of ownership the reason to change your advertising"). The Reel 2 caption narrates "Whitney then described…" in a WIA voice.

**Biggest weakness:** after the email and Reel 1, the voice turns into abstract principle-speak. The three captions repeat one idea in near-identical, wordy language.

### C: How we handle a new ad a client doesn't love (rank 3)

**W3 to W8 hits**
- W3, subject: "Can I say no to an ad my agency made?" is the reader's own question and concrete. This is the best subject in the set.
- W5/W7, covers: "Telling your ad team you don't love an ad" is specific. "The option to pass on a new ad" is acceptable. The hook "Can you be candid about a new ad?" is somewhat soft.
- W4, slide 1: "Your say in a new ad should include the option to decline it." It matches the title. Pass.
- W6: no insider terms.
- W8, b-roll: "New ads we'd made were still a choice." / "Whitney welcomed changes, or the choice to leave those ads unused." / "The work was already done. Approval still wasn't an obligation." It fails a cold viewer. The client's hesitation (the turn of the story) never appears on screen. Screen 1 is vague. Screen 2 names Whitney in the third person.

**Rule findings**
- The email has the send header, subjects, preview, bold line and "Talk soon, Whitney". It also uses Whitney's real words ("If you decide you don't want to use them at all, like no hard feelings there as well."), which is a good touch. However, it **names no offer and has no reply CTA**, both of which 4.3 requires. The opening scene is thin: "We'd sent some new ads for review. The answer should be a genuine choice." The client's reaction ("doesn't think he loves the new ads") is not told.
- Reel 1 is meant to be "the story, face to camera", but it generalizes: "When we send new ads for review, there are a couple of options." The story never appears in it.
- Two Reels with a stated reason. Carousel titled, 7 slides.
- An internal conflict: P1 says to keep "the client's situation and quotation out of public copy until clearance", but every asset is built on that situation.
- Voice: aphoristic and abstract ("The fact that someone has already made it shouldn't make that answer harder to give."; "There's room for respect and disagreement in a creative review.").

**Biggest weakness:** the story is gutted. Out of caution, the copy never tells what the client said, so the email and Reels argue the principle without the moment that proves it.

- W8: there is no b-roll Reel.

**Biggest weakness:** it would not publish. It uses the insider term "office hours" everywhere, has no concrete subject on any first touch, and lacks most of the email and asset skeleton.

## What separates the best from the rest

E and B are the only briefs that deliver the full 4.3 skeleton. Each email has a send header, a real opening scene, one bold line, a named offer with its descriptor, a reply CTA and "Talk soon, Whitney", and each brief has two Reels with a stated reason and a titled carousel whose slide 1 is that title. They also keep the actors exact (Carmen and "a member of the client's team"; "I explained… we planned"), with no invented outcome. E edges B because its story is concrete and visual: a nearby venue couples really compared, and another venue in a different market. B argues an abstract "scrutiny" principle at length.

C has the single best subject line ("Can I say no to an ad my agency made?"), but out of caution it never tells what the client said, so the assets argue the point without the moment. D, F and A lose most of the skeleton: no send header, one Reel, no carousel title, and missing offer or CTA. D refers to Whitney in the third person inside her own email. A repeats the insider term "office hours" across every asset.

The pattern matches section 3.3 point 4: the ideas are acceptable, and the differences come from the first touch, vocabulary and whether the story is actually told.
