## Fidelity breaks found

- 02 A, caption: "We hold every page we build to that standard before recommending it replace anything a client already has." This widens the scene into a universal protocol.
- 04 B, screen 2: "...before replacing them." This implies a replacement happened, or a winning page.
- 06 A, caption: "A client recently had that kind of reaction..." Coming right after a list of flaws, it implies the ads were poor.
- 07 B, caption: "I welcome feedback ... and also make clear that leaving those particular ads unused is an option." This turns one exchange into a standing practice.
- 13 B, opening: "...how they'd use the spaces and how the day would flow." This adds content to Whitney's prompt.

Some risks are carried by both versions, so they do not separate them:
- 01: the story is tied straight to ELEVATE, which comes close to naming the client's program.
- 07: the shared script says "When we send new ads for review, there are a couple of options", which generalizes one exchange.
- 09 and 10: an Accelerator offer follows a review story directly, which invites the inference that the client is a member.
- 11: the hooks credit Google Ads with what the client's team knew.

## Patterns across A and B (origin not guessed)

- **The differences sit at first touch and at the close.** In 05, 07, 09, 11, 12, 13 and 15 the bodies or scripts are identical or nearly so. Those pairs were decided by subjects, previews, hooks, covers, captions and last lines. In every one of those pairs except 11, which turned on the caption's last line, the winner was the version that names the concrete subject or the reader's question at first touch.
- **Paragraph slides appear on both sides.** In four of the six carousels one version writes 50 to 65-word paragraph slides: B in 02 and 06, A in 10 and 14. The stacked version won every carousel. A missing "We believe" slide goes with the paragraph style.
- **There are two registers.** One is concrete and reader-facing: photos, forms, how couples arrive, "You don't want to seem difficult". The other is abstract: "accounts", "contribution", "destination", "the distinction between delivering an asset and deciding the role it should play". The concrete register wins on voice. It also carries a small risk of adding detail beyond the sources (06 A, 13 B). The abstract register rarely breaks fidelity but reads like a policy memo and drops the actual moment (06 B, 07 B caption, 08 A).
- **The contract has misses on both sides.**
  - Emails with two subject options instead of three: B in 01 and 05, A in 13.
  - A missing both-readers slide: 02 A.
  - A British spelling: "recognising" in 02 B.
  - Emails with no reply CTA, in both versions: 05 and 13.
- **"Whitney" appears in the third person inside "we" carousels and captions** (02 B, 04, 06, 08 A). This sits awkwardly beside first-person signed captions in the same pack.
- **The contrast limit is often exceeded.** "Rather than", "without" and "isn't automatically" pile up, especially in the abstract versions. Slogan closes ("The same judgment should apply...", "candor belongs in the review") are concentrated in the losing versions.
- **No 20 October pattern recurs outright.** There are no third-party-narration subjects, no insider terms and no unanchored client lines. The nearest misses are bare-question or pronoun-led hooks (15 A, 11, 16 B) and a vague b-roll screen (08 A).
