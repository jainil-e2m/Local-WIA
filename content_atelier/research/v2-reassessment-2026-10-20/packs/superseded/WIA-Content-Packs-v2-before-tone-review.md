### What we changed, and why

Your feedback on the angles and on the 13 October packs said the same thing in different ways. We kept turning your conversations into lessons, when the content should show what you know, how you think, how you work with clients and why your approach matters.

So this time we started somewhere else. Before choosing anything, we studied how you build your own content: "The question we ask every new client," "It feels like our sales are on autopilot," the 10-week story, "What I taught my clients this week." Four things are true of every one of them, and they're true of every pack here:

1. **It starts from one real moment.** A sentence a client said, a question you ask every new client, a training you taught. One moment, told well, rather than a summary of a whole call.
2. **The moment is the proof, not the point.** The point is something WIA believes about how a wedding business grows, or what working with you is actually like.
3. **It's written for owners who are already doing the work.** Established, already investing, with a feeling there's more potential. Never someone in a place of desperation, and never a tutorial for fixing their own account.
4. **Each format opens the same idea through a different door.** The Reels, carousel and email repeat the idea, never the wording or the hook.

# Pack 1 · ATTRACT: We lowered her ad spend before launching anything new

## Angle card

**Content opportunity.** What we actually did in the first weeks with a new venue client this summer. Her budget had crept up without her leads getting better, so we brought spend down on both platforms, turned off the campaign spending the most, and rebuilt from a lower starting point. It shows how WIA thinks about money, and it pushes back on the most common advice in the industry: spend more.

**Strategic purpose.** ATTRACT · Founder-forward / industry authority. It's the idea in Savannah's strategy that "anyone who tells you… you have to spend more on ads… just wants your money," told through a real decision rather than as a slogan. It builds trust with owners who are wary of agencies. No offer CTA.

**Intended audience.** Established wedding pros already spending on Google or Meta (themselves, through an agency or a freelancer) whose budget has grown faster than their results. The example is a venue; the point applies to any wedding business.

**Core perspective.** More budget is something your ads earn, with proof. When we start with a new client, we look for what to stop before we look for what to scale. Start lower, find out what's genuinely working, then put money behind that.

**Tension.** The usual advice, and the platforms' own nudges, say the answer is a bigger budget. One of the first things we did for a new client was the opposite, and it came from an ads agency, which is the last place most people expect it.

**WIA expertise in action.**
- We asked how she felt about her budget. The venue's director told us her spend had "jumped up quite a bit" and traffic was up, but she wasn't "finding that there's, like, way better, stronger leads." She wanted it lower.
- Your call: don't slash it overnight, because that would be too drastic. But when a budget keeps climbing, Google "can sometimes just assume that you're just okay with paying more per lead," so reset with a fresh campaign at about half the daily budget.
- On Meta, the campaign spending the most had spent over $500 with no tracked leads. It had been built to find new inquiries only among people who had visited the website in the past 45 days. You turned it off on the call: "it doesn't make sense to spend just to spend."
- The team rebuilt Meta at a lower total budget, giving each campaign one job.
- Targeting was opened up to where her ideal couples actually come from, and the cities she knew sent price shoppers were left out.
- The plan, in your words: "start generating leads at a lower budget, lower cost per lead, and then start to scale up from there as we're starting to see good results."

**Evidence and claim boundaries.**
- Ads strategy call, new ELEVATE venue: 14:00 to 16:47 (budget); 31:03 to 35:40 (targeting and the campaign turned off); 27:05 to 42:37 (the rebuilt Meta structure).
- Her Google ads were performing well. We reset because the budget had crept up, not because Google was failing, so don't imply it was.
- No exact budgets in public copy. Don't name the venue.
- The Meta campaign was set up before we started working together. Don't criticise whoever built it.
- No result unless the venue agrees (see "Before you publish").

## Email

**SUBJECT:** We lowered her ad spend first
**PREVIEW TEXT:** Not what you'd expect from an ads agency, I know.

Hi {{contact.first_name}},

This summer we started working with a new venue client. On one of our very first strategy calls, we asked how she felt about her current ad budget, and her answer was one ads agencies don't usually love hearing: she'd love to bring it down.

Her budget had been creeping up over the past year. Her website traffic was up too. But she wasn't seeing better leads come out of it.

Honestly, that's a great instinct. Our answer was yes, just not all at once.

Her Google ads were actually performing well. But when a budget keeps climbing, Google can sometimes start to assume you're fine paying more for every lead. So instead of slashing the budget overnight, we brought it down to about half and started a fresh campaign, so we could see what her leads should really cost.

On Meta, the campaign spending the most money hadn't produced a single tracked lead. It had been set up before she started working with us, and it was trying to find all of her new inquiries among people who had visited her website in the last 45 days. That's a lot to ask of a very small group. We turned it off before the call was over. It doesn't make sense to spend just to spend.

Then we rebuilt her Meta ads at a lower total budget, and gave each campaign one job: introducing the venue to couples who'd never heard of it, asking for the inquiry, and staying in front of couples who were still deciding. She told us which nearby cities tend to send her price shoppers, so we left those out and pointed the ads at the places her ideal couples actually come from.

From there, the plan was simple. Start lower, find out what's genuinely working, and scale that.

To be clear, I'm a big believer in investing in your growth. I've seen what happens when wedding pros commit to their advertising instead of dabbling. But so much of the advice in our industry boils down to "spend more." More budget, more boosted posts, another platform. And the ad platforms will always suggest a bigger daily budget.

The wedding pros I see winning with ads aren't always the ones spending the most. They're the ones who know what their spend is actually doing, and who put more money behind whatever has earned it.

So here's my question for you. Is your ad budget higher than it was a year ago? And are your bookings?

Hit reply and tell me. I'd genuinely love to hear where you're at.

Talk soon,
Whitney
