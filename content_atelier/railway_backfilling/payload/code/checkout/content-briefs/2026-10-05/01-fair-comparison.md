## Strategic assignment

**Audience:** Established venues and wedding professionals running ads who make website decisions from small weekly lead counts.

**Objective:** Help owners replace reactive edits with a clear, testable question and ask more useful questions about their reports.

**Funnel & pillar:** Warm · Education · Strategic decision-making

**Reader promise:** Leave with a simple way to decide what a landing-page result tells you, what it leaves unanswered, and which question to test next.

**Belief shift:** From “fewer leads means the form needs fixing” to “first establish what changed and whether the comparison supports that explanation.”

**What makes this angle distinct:** This is about interpreting a comparison before changing a marketing variable. Keep it separate from the testimonial angle’s message placement and the footage angle’s creative selection.

**Natural offer fit:** Accelerators. The final invitation follows the CTA direction below.

## Core insight

A marketing result is useful when you understand the comparison behind it. Check what changed, the traffic source, and the strength of the evidence before deciding what to fix.

## Audience pain

You invest in ads, see a quieter few days, and feel pressure to change the form, page, and campaign at once without knowing which part needs attention.

## Message spine

1. Start with the same form on two pages and the temptation to shorten it after fewer leads.
2. Separate the observation from the explanation: page, audience, time window, and tracking each matter.
3. Compare like with like, check the full inquiry-to-tour journey, and change one deliberate variable.
4. An early lead count can suggest the next question; a stronger comparison supports the next decision.

## Narrative framework

**Hook:** Open on the team considering a form change, followed by Whitney checking that both forms were identical.

**Problem:** A quieter week creates pressure to change several things at once. Recognise that pressure without shaming the owner or the team.

**Main point:** A result and an explanation are different. Check page differences, audience, traffic source, observation window, and tracking before choosing a fix.

**Transformation:** The owner can identify what remains uncertain and make one deliberate next decision rather than chase each weekly fluctuation.

**CTA:** Ask the reader which change they are evaluating and what they expect it to improve.

## Evidence boundaries

### What the meetings establish

- Whitney said the forms were identical and requested a comparable observation window in the Google review.
- The team reported early lead/click counts and questioned whether enough time had passed.
- The separate Meta review described a different early picture for the same client.

### Proposed interpretations and tests

- The page, audience, or measurement could explain the observed difference; the export does not isolate causation.
- A more controlled comparison could support a better decision. Equal elapsed time alone is insufficient.

### Claims this brief does not support

- One landing page statistically beat the other.
- Shortening this form would improve conversion.
- Eight days is a universal testing rule, or a higher inquiry rate guarantees more bookings.

### Email

A founder reflection on the identical-form moment, moving from a plausible fix to a more useful question.

**Reader job:** Recognise a reactive decision in their own reporting and describe a clearer test.

**Length:** 350–500 words; one observation, one practical diagnostic sequence, and one reply invitation.

**Subject-line directions — proposed, not approved copy:**

- The form was the same
- What changed before the leads changed?
- Before you edit your landing page

**Opening direction:** Describe the internal Google review anonymously. Start with the proposed form change and the discovery that the forms matched. Omit exact counts and client identity in the default draft.

**Outline:**

1. Make the pressure familiar: a few slower days can make every part of the funnel look suspect.
2. Use the same-form finding to separate an observation from an explanation.
3. Walk through three questions: what actually changed, who saw each page, and whether tracking and observation are comparable.
4. Acknowledge the Meta counterpoint without combining its results with Google.
5. End with the reader’s next test, not a declared winner or a promised improvement.

**Email CTA:** Invite a reply naming one landing-page change and the question it is intended to answer.

### Carousel

An eight-slide comparison checklist that turns a quiet week into a testable question.

**Reader job:** Save a decision aid and use it before changing a live page.

**Slide 1 · Hook — Before you fix the form…**

Introduce the question: does the comparison support that fix?

**Visual direction:** A simple page outline and one highlighted question; no performance number.

**Slide 2 · Problem — Fewer leads is an observation**

Show how a result can lead to several plausible explanations. Avoid treating one explanation as fact.

**Visual direction:** Observation card branching to page, audience, timing, and tracking.

**Slide 3 · Main point — Check what actually changed**

Use the identical-form moment. Inspect the pages before acting on a form recommendation.

**Visual direction:** Two illustrative form outlines labelled “example”; identical fields highlighted.

**Slide 4 · Main point — Compare the audience and channel**

Cold Google traffic and Meta retargeting answer different questions. Keep their observations separate.

**Visual direction:** Two clearly labelled audience paths; no invented results.

**Slide 5 · Main point — Check the window and tracking**

Comparable periods, enough observations, and consistent inquiry definitions matter. Same number of days is not proof.

**Visual direction:** A checklist, not a fabricated significance chart.

**Slide 6 · Transformation — Follow the inquiry further**

Ask about relevance and the next sales step, such as a tour or consultation; do not declare quality from volume alone.

**Visual direction:** Inquiry → review → tour/consultation → booking, with uncertainty shown.

**Slide 7 · Transformation — Choose one next question**

Write down the variable, the expected effect, and the evidence needed before changing several things.

**Visual direction:** A blank three-field decision card the reader can use.

**Slide 8 · CTA — What are you trying to learn?**

Invite readers to save the checklist and describe the page decision they are evaluating.

**Visual direction:** A single readable invitation; no sales button or unconfirmed URL.

**Caption direction:** Explain that this checklist comes from a team-review lesson, not a winning-page case study. Add the uncertainty about early comparisons and one invitation to save or comment.

### Reel 1 · The identical-form moment

Use one observation to show why a plausible fix still needs checking.

**Role:** Challenge the diagnosis

**Length:** 45–60 seconds; aim for approximately 95–130 spoken words, then time the read. Captions and production notes are separate from spoken copy.

**Hook direction:** Start with the surprise that the proposed form fix concerned two identical forms.

**Spoken argument beats — develop into a script after brief approval:**

1. Recognise the temptation to shorten a form after fewer inquiries.
2. Reveal what Whitney checked.
3. Explain why checking the proposed cause comes before choosing a tactic.

**Visual direction:** Founder to camera; cut to two fictional form cards. No live account data.

**Reel CTA:** Ask what the viewer checks first when inquiries slow.

### Reel 2 · What makes the comparison useful?

Demonstrate why time, audience, and measurement must be read together.

**Role:** Explain the evidence

**Length:** 45–60 seconds; aim for approximately 95–130 spoken words, then time the read. Captions and production notes are separate from spoken copy.

**Hook direction:** Ask whether two equal-length windows automatically make a fair comparison.

**Spoken argument beats — develop into a script after brief approval:**

1. Name audience and channel as variables.
2. Explain why equal time does not ensure sufficient or comparable observations.
3. End with the question the available evidence can actually answer.

**Visual direction:** Founder with three cards: audience, observation, tracking. No statistical tutorial or invented chart.

**Reel CTA:** Invite viewers to save the three checks for their next report.

### Reel 3 · Write the test before the edit

Help the viewer turn one proposed page edit into a clear test question.

**Role:** Give a practical action

**Length:** 45–60 seconds; aim for approximately 95–130 spoken words, then time the read. Captions and production notes are separate from spoken copy.

**Hook direction:** Ask the viewer to finish: “I am changing this because…”

**Spoken argument beats — develop into a script after brief approval:**

1. Choose one variable.
2. State the expected effect and a consistent way to observe it.
3. Keep downstream inquiry quality in view and avoid changing several variables together.

**Visual direction:** Show a handwritten example labelled illustrative; keep it simple enough to read.

**Reel CTA:** Invite a comment describing one change and its intended purpose.
