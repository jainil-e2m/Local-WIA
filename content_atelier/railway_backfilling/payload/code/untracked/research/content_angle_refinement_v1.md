- Identified the right thought but prioritized the operational details.
- Interpreted the task as producing useful advice rather than identifying compelling content.
- Was instructed to explain, educate, or provide practical takeaways too prominently.
- Used an editorial rubric that rewarded completeness and utility over creative distinction.
- Produced a promising idea that was subsequently weakened by another stage.
- Had insufficient examples demonstrating the difference between a topic, an operational lesson, and a compelling angle.
- Could identify strong framing but failed to select it reliably.
- Was simply unable to make the required distinction consistently with the tested model or configuration.

These are competing hypotheses. Test them rather than declaring one of them the root cause in advance.

### Perform a source-to-angle transformation audit

For several strong and weak outputs, create a traceable comparison:

| Step | Question |
|---|---|
| Source moment | What actually happened or was said? |
| Underlying thought | What is the most important point or tension in that moment? |
| Initial model interpretation | What did the model believe the material was about? |
| Generated framing | What did it choose to make the content about? |
| Lost or preserved signal | Did the original point of view survive the transformation? |
| Final selection | Why did that candidate survive or fail? |
| Root cause | What observable instruction, behavior, or mechanism explains the result? |

Where the underlying thought is ambiguous, preserve that ambiguity rather than pretending there is only one correct interpretation.

The purpose is to discover whether the failure is primarily a problem of comprehension, salience, creative generation, editorial discrimination, or selection.

---

## 4. Investigate Why the Previous Reviewer Failed

Do not assume that adding an editorial reviewer is either the solution or inherently ineffective.

The previous investigation reported that reviewer calls rejected promising ideas when implementation details were missing even though the central thought did not require those details.

Investigate why.

For actual reviewer examples, determine:

- What the reviewer was instructed to optimize.
- What criteria or output schema it was given.
- Whether it could distinguish the appeal of an idea from the completeness of its supporting explanation.
- Whether it was evaluating the angle or an imagined full Reel script.
- Whether it required evidence for the central thought or unnecessarily required evidence for every possible elaboration.
- Whether it treated ambiguity as a flaw even when the ambiguity was intentional or harmless.
- Whether it had access to the original source passage and sufficient surrounding context.
- Whether its evaluation was calibrated against strong approved angles and weak generated examples.
- Whether it could explain why one candidate was creatively superior to another.
- Whether its judgments changed depending on model, prompt, candidate ordering, or available context.

Identify whether the reviewer failed because of its instructions, its evidence requirements, its model capability, its task definition, or a combination of factors.

### Establish the correct evidence standard

The system must distinguish two things:

**Evidence required to support the central angle.** The core idea and any factual claim presented as true must be defensible from the source material.

**Details required to write a complete script.** These may be unnecessary during angle selection if the angle can be communicated without them.

For example, an angle about a professional position can be valid without a complete performance analysis, a finalized process, or a proven business outcome.

Conversely, an angle must not invent a result, fabricate a client opinion, or turn an unresolved question into a verified success.

Determine how these two standards should be reflected in the generation and evaluation stages.

Do not remove editorial evaluation solely because a particular reviewer implementation performed poorly. First establish what an evaluator would actually need to judge well and whether that function has to be implemented as a separate model call.

---

## 5. Determine What the Current Pipeline Is Actually Optimizing

Inspect the complete existing OpenRouter pipeline and its current prompts after the previous modifications.

Trace what the system rewards at each stage.

In particular, determine whether the effective objective is inadvertently to maximize:

- Number of angles produced.
- Coverage of transcript subjects.
- Amount of actionable advice.
- Educational completeness.
- Clarity of explanation.
- Evidence completeness.
- Agreement between multiple model calls.
- Novelty relative to previous posts.
- Similarity to approved content.
- Explicit rubric compliance.
- Or actual creative appeal and the likelihood that an angle deserves publication.

Do not assume what a prompt optimizes from its wording alone. Examine the generated outputs and the selection behavior.

Look for conflicts between instructions, such as asking for distinctive social content while also requiring comprehensive takeaways, detailed explanations, multiple lessons, strict evidence coverage, or fixed numbers of ideas.

Investigate whether JSON schemas, fixed output fields, model token limits, prompt ordering, context compression, or post-processing inadvertently favor explanatory and operational writing.

Determine whether the different OpenRouter stages reinforce one another's biases.

For example, if the first stage turns a source moment into a lesson, and the subsequent stage is asked only to make that lesson clearer, the later stage may never recover the original creative opportunity.

Establish the actual mechanisms at work before proposing any changes.

---

## 6. Separate Prompt Failure From Model Capability and Workflow Failure

The previous investigation did not establish the precise contribution of model capability. Resolve this uncertainty as far as the available evidence permits.

Design targeted experiments that isolate the most consequential hypotheses.

Where practical, compare:

**Test A — Baseline reproduction**

Run the actual existing generation path against the same transcripts and preserve all available intermediate outputs.

**Test B — Source interpretation only**

Ask the model to identify underlying thoughts, tensions, decisions, and potentially interesting source moments, without generating final angles.

Determine whether the creative opportunities are already lost at this stage.

**Test C — Framing only**

Supply a carefully identified source moment and its underlying thought. Ask the same model to generate alternative audience-facing angles without changing the source or surrounding configuration.

This helps establish whether the model can frame a known opportunity well when the source-selection problem is removed.

**Test D — Editorial discrimination**

Provide a mixture of strong and weak angles and ask the model to make pairwise editorial comparisons using an explicit definition of creative quality.

Test whether it can distinguish a compelling point of view from a routine lesson, rather than merely assign high scores to every candidate.

**Test E — Selection and filtering**

Use the same candidate pool and compare the existing selection logic with the proposed selection process.

Determine whether promising ideas are being discarded or weaker ones are being promoted.

**Test F — Model and configuration comparison**

Only after the previous tests establish where the failure occurs, compare the relevant available models and supported configurations on the specific failed task.

Avoid changing prompts, models, context, and generation parameters simultaneously unless a factorial experiment is genuinely warranted.

For each experiment, specify:

- The hypothesis.
- The independent variable.
- The controls.
- The exact source material.
- The evaluation method.
- The expected observation if the hypothesis is correct.
- The observation that would weaken or falsify it.
- The results.
- The limitations of the conclusion.

Use repeated trials where output variability could materially affect the conclusion.

Do not waste calls on broad experiments that cannot distinguish between the competing explanations.

If the available artifacts or environment prevent an experiment from being performed, document the limitation rather than inventing a result.

---

## 7. Define What a Successful Fix Must Actually Achieve

Do not equate a better-sounding explanation, a larger number of prompts, or increased agreement between models with a successful fix.

The desired behavior is that the system repeatedly:

1. Recognizes promising moments within the transcript.
2. Identifies their underlying meaning and audience relevance.
3. Develops compelling and meaningfully different creative framings.
4. Preserves the central point of view rather than reducing everything to practical instruction.
5. Differentiates strong candidates from merely relevant ones.
6. Selects a small number of genuinely worthwhile ideas.
7. Keeps factual claims grounded in available evidence.
8. Avoids redundant angles and maintains useful content diversity.
9. Explains why each selected angle deserves its slot.
10. Does not claim success when the available candidates do not meet the required standard.

### Build an observable editorial rubric

Use the reference angles and actual failure cases to establish explicit evaluation criteria.

At minimum, assess:

- Strength and distinctiveness of the central thought.
- Natural audience relevance.
- Quality of creative framing.
- Presence of a clear point of view, observation, or meaningful tension where appropriate.
- Suitability for a short Reel.
- Consistency with the approved content corpus.
- Appropriate specificity and educational depth.
- Support for the central claim from the source evidence.
- Editorial distinctiveness relative to other candidates.
- Absence of generic, redundant, unnecessarily technical, or overexplained framing.

Define concrete examples of passing, borderline, and failing behavior.

Avoid simplistic universal rules such as requiring every angle to be controversial, emotional, opinionated, or humorous. A thoughtful question, recognizable situation, commercial observation, or human interaction may be equally strong.

Do not establish arbitrary numerical thresholds and present them as validated standards. Use initial evaluation results to calibrate the rubric.

If a score is used, explain its meaning and validate whether it helps distinguish strong from weak content.

---

## 8. Investigate How to Make the Outcome More Reliable

I want a solution that maximizes the likelihood of consistently strong editorial output.

Investigate the difference between guaranteeing a response format and guaranteeing creative quality.

A schema can require certain fields, but it cannot ensure that the ideas inside them are interesting. A quality score can appear precise without being a reliable measurement. Multiple model calls can agree and still make the same mistake.

Design a reliability strategy that combines the best supported measures rather than assuming a single prompt can solve everything.

Consider the following possibilities and test their value:

### A. Better candidate discovery

Generate candidate moments before generating final angles, while retaining enough transcript context to interpret each moment accurately.

### B. Alternative creative framings

Generate multiple meaningfully different framings for promising moments rather than committing immediately to the first plausible idea.

Avoid generating several superficial rewrites of the same concept.

### C. Comparative judgment

Compare candidates against one another using the specific qualities that distinguish the reference angles from the actual failure cases.

Prefer comparative decisions that explain what makes one candidate stronger over generic numeric ratings without meaningful justification.

### D. Editorial calibration

Use selected approved scripts and actual weak examples to teach transferable differences in creative framing.

Determine how many examples are useful and how to prevent copying or overfitting to them.

### E. Independent evaluation

Where useful, separate candidate creation from candidate assessment. Test whether the evaluator can reliably recognize quality before making it a permanent stage.

The evaluator must not reject a strong idea simply because irrelevant implementation details or full-script information are missing.

### F. Iterative improvement

Allow weak candidates to receive a targeted revision based on the specific deficiency identified, rather than asking the model to rewrite everything or blindly repeat the same generation request.

Limit retries, preserve previous candidates, and stop when the quality criteria are satisfied or further attempts are unlikely to help.

### G. Evidence validation

Verify the central claim and source provenance without requiring every potential supporting detail to appear in the angle.

### H. Quality-gated finalization

Establish what happens when no candidate passes the editorial standard.

The system should preserve the ability to return fewer angles, request human editorial input, or mark the run for review rather than quietly promoting weak ideas to satisfy a quota.

### I. Reliability monitoring

Record model identity, prompt version, source identifiers, stage outputs, evaluation outcomes, and relevant request configuration so that future failures can be diagnosed rather than guessed at.

Choose the simplest architecture that demonstrates a meaningful improvement. Do not introduce more calls merely because a multi-stage workflow sounds rigorous.

---

## 9. Establish What Can and Cannot Be Guaranteed

Address this explicitly in the report.

I do not want a false promise that a prompt, a particular model, or a reviewer can guarantee the exact intended creative outcome on every run.

Separate the requirements into three groups:

**Hard guarantees:** Properties the application can enforce, such as valid output structure, source references being present, duplicate checks passing, required provenance being recorded, or a result not being marked approved until acceptance checks pass.

**Statistical reliability targets:** Properties whose consistency can be measured over repeated generations, such as the proportion of outputs judged to meet the editorial standard.

**Human editorial decisions:** Matters involving taste, brand judgment, and ambiguous creative value that cannot be conclusively automated by a model score.

Determine what guarantees are feasible within the current architecture and what requires additional application logic.

Design the pipeline so that it guarantees process integrity and avoids silently accepting known failures, while measuring and improving the probability of creative success.

Do not label a system reliable merely because it produces an output every time. A system that correctly flags insufficient quality is preferable to one that always produces a predetermined number of weak angles.

---

## 10. Validate the Solution Against Unseen Source Material

Do not tune the entire system exclusively to the six reference angles.

Use Week 3 and Week 4 as an initial benchmark because they produced the reference output. Then identify additional accessible weeks or transcripts suitable for testing.

Separate the material used to develop prompts from the material used to evaluate whether the improvements generalize.

If sufficient data is available:

- Use one set of transcripts and reference examples for prompt development.
- Use different transcript material for validation.
- Include both strong and weak generated candidates.
- Evaluate outputs without telling the evaluator which approach produced them, where practical.
- Compare results against the existing baseline.
- Run repeated generations when variability matters.
- Assess whether the revised process consistently avoids the known failure modes.
- Check for regression into repetitive, formulaic, educational, or unsupported angles.

Measure quality using the same editorial rubric throughout the comparison.

Report the actual denominator, scores or decisions, failures, and limitations. Do not rely only on illustrative success stories.

If there are no independently approved scripts for the additional weeks, distinguish editorial evaluation from demonstrated real-world approval.

## 11. Deliver the Findings Before Making Further Changes

For this investigation, do not immediately rewrite the prompts, introduce new model calls, or modify production behavior.

First complete the forensic analysis and present a root-cause report with the following sections:

### A. Executive diagnosis

What is the most strongly supported explanation for the current failure? How confident are we, and what evidence supports that conclusion?

### B. Audit of the previous solution

What did the 16 calls actually establish? What was changed in the implementation? Which conclusions were justified, and which remain unproven?

### C. Failure trace

Trace several actual source moments through candidate creation, creative framing, evaluation, and final selection. Identify exactly where quality was gained or lost.

### D. Ranked root causes

For each proposed cause, provide supporting evidence, contradictory evidence, confidence, and the experiment needed to establish or reject it.

Do not manufacture certainty where the evidence is inconclusive.

### E. Reviewer diagnosis

Explain why the previous editorial review was unreliable and whether the underlying task is feasible with a better-defined evaluation procedure.

### F. Root-cause test plan

Specify the smallest set of targeted experiments that could distinguish between the remaining plausible explanations.

### G. Reliability design

Explain how to combine better generation, comparative selection, source validation, revision, and quality gates into an effective workflow. Distinguish validated improvements from proposed mechanisms that still need testing.

### H. Acceptance criteria

Define measurable requirements for the next implementation, including an editorial-quality target calibrated from observed results, a source-grounding requirement, diversity requirements, and behavior when no candidate passes.

### I. Implementation plan

Rank recommended changes by expected impact, strength of evidence, cost, latency, complexity, and risk of introducing new failure modes.

Identify the minimum effective change before considering a more elaborate solution.

**Do not modify the application during this diagnostic phase.** Preserve the implementation and baseline artifacts until the report explains the root cause and specifies how a proposed fix will be tested.

Once the diagnosis is complete, stop and present the findings. The implementation should be a subsequent, evidence-driven step rather than an assumption built into the investigation.

## Final objective

We are not trying to force OpenRouter models to imitate six example sentences.

We are trying to reproduce a deeper editorial capability: recognizing which part of a real conversation contains a worthwhile thought, finding the most compelling way to communicate it to the intended audience, and distinguishing that idea from the many technically valid but creatively weaker alternatives.

The investigation must determine why the current pipeline loses that capability, which intervention is most likely to restore it, and how to demonstrate that improvement through controlled evidence.

The final recommendation must be specific enough to implement, measurable enough to validate, and honest enough to distinguish an enforceable guarantee from a probabilistic improvement.

Do not recommend another generic prompt rewrite unless the evidence shows that prompt design is the actual root cause.
